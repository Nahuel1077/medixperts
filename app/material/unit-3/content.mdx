<Presentation
list={["Lesson 1: Advanced active-attentive listening (Video):"]}
/>
Introduces candidates to the fundamental cognitive shift required for professional interpreting: moving from passive hearing to active-attentive listening.

<Presentation
list={["Lesson 2: Short-term memory techniques (Video):"]}
/>
Addresses the physiological limits of human memory and provides interpreters with cognitive hacks to safely bypass them. Short-term memory naturally holds only about seven items for up to 30 seconds, yet medical providers often speak for 45 seconds straight. To safely bridge this gap, the lesson introduces the three pillars of memory enhancement.

<Presentation
list={["Lesson 3: Note-taking (different techniques and methods) (Written):"]} 
/>
Explains why note-taking for interpreters is not a literal transcript; it is a minimalist visual map designed to trigger short-term memory. This written lesson outlines the mechanics of professional notation, teaching students to capture concepts rather than individual words. It focuses on three golden rules: conceptual representation (using symbols and diagrams), vertical layout (writing from top to bottom rather than left to right to separate subjects, verbs, and objects), and shift indentation (placing modifying details directly underneath main ideas to represent logical dependencies visually).

<Presentation
list={["Lesson 4: Accurate message reformulation (Video):"]}
/>
Teaches interpreters how to reformulate messages accurately without falling into the "literal translation trap," which often leads to clinical nonsense. It covers the crucial balance between literal accuracy—preserving the exact meaning, formal register, tone, and clinical intent of the source speech—and dynamic equivalence, which ensures the translated message sounds entirely natural in the target language.

<Presentation
list={["Lesson 5: Managing the session and interruptions (Video):"]}
/>
Teaches students how to actively manage the pace of a session and handle interruptions when communication begins to break down. It highlights the three most common conversational disruptions: over-talking (where a doctor or patient speaks for several minutes without pausing, overwhelming the interpreter's memory), simultaneous speaking (where both parties talk at the same time, distorting the audio), and side conversations (where the patient whispers or argues with family members, leaving the provider excluded).

<Presentation
list={["Lesson 6: What to do when you don’t understand something (intervention skills) (Video):"]}
/>
Focuses on a critical reality of the job: what to do when you do not understand a term, phrase, or instruction. The golden rule taught here is the Rule of Absolute Transparency. Interpreters must never guess, assume, or omit information to cover up their confusion.

<Presentation 
list={["Lesson 7: Controlling the flow of communication (Written):"]}
/>
Controlling the flow of communication is the art of pacing speakers so you can deliver a high-quality, accurate interpretation. This written guide provides students with proactive, practical strategies to manage conversational speed without making the provider or the patient feel rushed or restricted.

<Presentation
list={["Lesson 8: Interpreter protocol and essential call language (Written):"]}
/>
Details the precise, mandatory language required by major Language Service Providers (LSPs). In remote medical interpreting, delivering consistent, scripted protocols is essential to passing quality audits and maintaining a professional structure. 

<Presentation
list={["Lesson 9: Handling numbers, dates, dosages, and spelling (Video):"]}
/>
In medical settings, numbers, dates, dosages, and drug spellings carry extreme clinical risk. Confusing "15 mg" with "50 mg" or misinterpreting a patient's date of birth can lead to life-threatening errors. This lesson teaches students how to handle high-risk data with zero margin for error. It introduces the "Write-First Protocol," a strict rule requiring interpreters to physically write down any number, date, or dosage the exact millisecond they hear it, bypassing their cognitive processing and documenting it mechanically on paper before interpreting.
